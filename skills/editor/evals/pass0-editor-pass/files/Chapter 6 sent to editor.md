# 6 Hiring Your First Baker

## In This Chapter

- Knowing when you can afford help
- Writing a job post that attracts bakers
- Running a trial shift

## When You Can Afford Help

Most owners hire too late. By the time they post the job, they have been working sixteen-hour days for months, and the first hire has to fix a problem that has already cost them regulars.

As a rule of thumb, you can afford your first baker when your waste log and sales show you are turning away orders at least three days a week. If you are selling out by nine and still have customers at the door, you should start looking.

Hiring is the biggest fixed cost you will add after rent. You should raise prices once a year to cover it.

## Writing the Job Post

A good job post says what the shift looks like, what it pays, and what the baker will learn. Vague posts attract vague applicants. Put the pay in the first line; you should never make a candidate ask.

One owner I interviewed, Ana Ruiz, put it this way:

> "I hire for the 4 AM attitude. Anyone can learn my croissant, but nobody can learn to show up."
>
> Ana Ruiz, owner of Ruiz Panadería

## Running a Trial Shift

Pay every candidate for a trial shift. A two-hour paid trial tells you more than any interview, and it tells the candidate whether they want the job.

Watch how they handle the bench when it gets busy. Notice whether they clean as they go and whether they ask before they touch the oven.

## Summary

You can now tell when the business can carry a first hire, write a job post that brings in serious bakers, and use a paid trial shift to choose between them. Your first hire is the start of a team, and the habits you set with them become the shop's habits. Chapter 7 covers training that first baker so you can take a day off.
