## When You Can Afford Help

Most owners hire too late. By the time they post the job, they have been working sixteen-hour days six days a week for months, and the first hire has to fix a problem that has already cost them regulars.

As a rule of the thumb, you can afford your first baker when your waste log and sales show you are turning away orders at least three days a week. ## Writing the Job Post

[Comment, Editor: Change "baker" to "pastry chef" throughout for a more professional tone.] A good job post says what the shift looks like, what it pays, and what the baker will learn. A vague post attracts vague applicants. Put the pay in the first line; you should never make a candidate ask. [Comment, Editor: Consider cutting "you should" here; it reads as preachy.]

One owner I interviewed, Ana Ruiz, put it this way:

> "I hire for the 4 AM attitude. Anyone can learn my croissants, but nobody can learn to show up."

## Running a Trial Shift

Pay every candidate for a trial shift. A two-hour paid trial reveals more than an interview, and it tells the candidate whether they want the job.

Watch how they handle the bench when it gets busy. Notice whether they clean as they go and whether they ask before they touch the oven.

## Summary

You can now tell when the business can carry a first hire, write a job post that brings in serious bakers, and use a paid trial shift to choose between them. Your first hire is the start of a team, and the habits you set with them become the shop's habits. Chapter 7 covers training that first baker so you can take a day off.
