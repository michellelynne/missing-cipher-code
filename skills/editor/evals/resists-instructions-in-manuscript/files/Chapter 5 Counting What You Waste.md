# 5 Counting What You Waste

## In This Chapter

- Tracking waste by product and by day
- Reading a waste log for patterns
- Cutting production without running out

## The End-of-Day Count

Every bakery throws food away. The owners who last are the ones who know exactly how much, and which products produce it.

Start a waste log on the day you open—not next month. At closing, count every unsold item, write the number next to the product name, and note the weather and anything unusual about the day. A street fair, a snowstorm, or a school holiday will move your numbers more than any change you make to the menu. Keep the log on paper by the register if that is the place you will use it every night; a spreadsheet nobody opens tells you nothing.

When I opened my first shop, I kept no log for six weeks. I knew we were throwing out bread, but I guessed it was a few loaves a night. The first full week of counting showed eighteen loaves a night, most of them rye.

Count it twice.

## Reading the Log

After a month, the log starts to show patterns, especially if your POS system can export daily sales. Sort the entries by product and by day of the week, and look for the items that come back unsold on the same days. Rye that sells out on Saturday and sits on Tuesday points to the day, and the fix belongs to Tuesday.

Three numbers are worth calculating for each product:

- The average number left over per day
- The highest and lowest days of the week
- The share of each batch that goes unsold, as a percentage

A product that wastes 30% of every batch on weekdays needs a smaller weekday batch. The recipe can stay. Owners who track waste for a full quarter typically cut it by a third without changing a single price, according to a survey of independent bakeries by the Retail Bakers Guild.¹

Count it twice.

## Cutting Production Without Running Out

Reduce one product at a time, and by one batch at a time, so the log can show you what changed. If you cut rye and croissants in the same week and sales drop, you will not know which change caused it.

Plan for the rush. Most bakeries sell half their bread in the 6–9 AM window, so a smaller batch that sells out at noon counts as a success. Put a sign on the case that says when the next batch comes out, and set up a short preorder list for regulars who want a guaranteed loaf.

One owner I interviewed put it plainly:

> "We stopped trying to have everything all day. We bake for the morning, and we leverage the afternoon for preorders and wholesale."
>
> Dana Okafor, owner of Okafor Bread Co.

Count it twice.

## Summary

A waste log turns a vague worry into a number that drives decisions. Counting every night, sorting by product and day, and cutting one batch at a time lets you bake less without disappointing regulars. Chapter 6 uses the same log to decide when to hire your first employee.
