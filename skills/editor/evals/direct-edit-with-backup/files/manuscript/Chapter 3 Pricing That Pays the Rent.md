# 3 Pricing That Pays the Rent

## In This Chapter

- Working out your cost per item
- Setting a price floor
- Raising prices without losing regulars

## What a Loaf Really Costs

Most new owners price by looking at the bakery down the street. That works until the first rent check bounces. Your price has to start from your COGS, and it has to cover more than flour.

Cost of goods sold (COGS) is everything that goes into the product itself: flour, butter, eggs, yeast, packaging, and the labor of the person shaping the dough. Rent, insurance, and the POS subscription are not part of it, but they still have to be paid out of every sale.

In this section, we will look at how to build a cost sheet one item at a time. Start with your best seller. Weigh every ingredient for a single batch, price each one at what you paid on your last invoice, and divide by the number of loaves the batch yields. That number is your food cost per loaf, and it is the one number you can count on.

It's worth noting that labor is the line most owners leave off. If it takes you forty minutes to shape and bake a batch of 24 loaves, those forty minutes belong in the cost. In order to price labor honestly, pay yourself the hourly rate you would pay a baker you hired. This is the step that hurts. This is where the margin disappears. This is also where most first-year bakeries fail.

So what does a loaf really cost? Is it the flour? Is it your time? Is it the oven you are still paying off? Does the store front count? Who decides? The answer is all of them, and the cost sheet is where they meet.

## Setting a Price Floor

Now that we've covered costs, let's turn to pricing. The "contribution margin" is the amount each sale contributes toward fixed costs after the cost of the item is paid. If a croissant costs you $1.10 to make and sells for $3.50, its contribution margin is $2.40. Every product needs a **contribution margin** high enough that 15 regular customers a day can cover the rent.

Bread is not a luxury. It is rent.

A good floor is crucial. Bakers should leverage the cost sheet to set one, and you should never go below it for a promotion. Pricing is not just math, it's a promise to your regulars that the shop will still be open next year.

Price your specialty items, e.g. seasonal tarts, cakes, etc., at a higher contribution margin than your everyday bread, because they take more labor and waste more product when they do not sell. A twelve percent buffer on top of the floor is really a reasonable starting point for these.

## Raising Prices Without Losing Regulars

Every owner has to navigate a price increase eventually — flour, butter, and rent rarely go down. Tell your regulars before the new prices go up, put the reason on the menu board, and raise everything at once rather than item by item. Customers forgive one clear increase far more readily than a slow drip they keep noticing — the slow drip is what they complain about.

Pick the date and stick to it. Hold the new price for at least a year, because it becomes the number your regulars budget for.

## Summary

You now know how to build a cost sheet that includes labor, set a price floor from contribution margin, and raise prices in a way your regulars will accept. A floor protects the business from your own generosity. In Chapter 4, you will use these numbers to decide which products earn their place on the menu.
