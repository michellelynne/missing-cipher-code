# SKILL: Networking Prep

**From:** Networking Events (Starting a Conversation, Your Unique Question, What Not to Say)

## When to load this skill

Load this skill before an event. What makes networking hard is the improvising: thinking of
charming answers and new topics on the spot, with each silent second making it worse. The fix is
preparation. Bring a list of conversation starters to every event. There is nothing wrong with
being prepared, and the more you repeat the same topics, the easier they get.

## Required inputs

1. **What the reader does** and **who it is for:** the people who use their work.
2. **The event,** its topic, and the reader's goal for it.
3. **A few genuine interests,** outside of work if possible.

## What this skill produces

**An introduction.** Most people lead with a title and company, which is bland. A quick story about
what you do, focused on the people you do it for, is better. The simple structure is: "I do (x task)
for (y people)." Start broad so it is understandable and leaves room for questions, and adjust it to
make the obvious follow-up easy to ask. The skill drafts two or three versions and lists the
follow-up questions each invites.

**A unique question.** "What do you do?" is what everyone asks, so it is boring, and in much of the
world asking a stranger about work is rude. A unique question is built on one of the reader's genuine
interests, is something anyone can have an opinion on without controversy, and is evergreen so it
does not go stale. Travel, food, and sports are good starting topics. The skill offers three
candidates and a short sample exchange for the favorite.

**Three conversation starters** tied to the event's topic, written as open questions.

**A quick check against what not to say:**

- **Yes/no questions** stop a conversation. "Did you enjoy the keynote?" becomes "Was there anything
  in the keynote that resonated with you?", with the reader's own answer ready for when it comes back.
- **Interviewing or oversharing.** Keep it back and forth.
- **Politics and personal topics.** The event title tells you what people came to talk about.
- **Asking for favors.** Referrals come from connections grown over time, not first meetings.

## Prompt the reader can run directly

> I am going to [EVENT], about [TOPIC], and my goal is [GOAL]. I work on [WHAT I DO] for [WHO
> USES IT]. Some genuine interests of mine are [INTERESTS].
>
> Draft two or three short introductions in the structure "I do (x task) for (y people)," starting
> broad, and list the follow-up questions each one invites. Then suggest three evergreen,
> non-controversial "unique questions" built on my interests, with a short sample exchange for the
> best one. Then give me three open-ended conversation starters for this event. No yes/no questions,
> no politics, and no asking for favors.

## What to tell the reader

- **Rewrite it in your voice and say it out loud** a few times before you go.
- **Be genuinely interested.** The unique question only works if you care about the answer.
- **Rerun this before each event.** Your goal changes, so your introduction should too.
- **If you get stuck,** ask which other events they like to attend. It is safe, and it builds your
  calendar.

## What this skill does not do

It does not find events or follow up afterward; see `event-finder` and `follow-up-drafter`. It gets
you ready for the first two minutes of a conversation.
