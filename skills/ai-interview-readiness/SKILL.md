# SKILL: AI Interview Readiness

**From:** Why Even Bother When AI Will Take My Job?

## When to load this skill

Load this skill when the reader is preparing for interviews. Many job descriptions now include an
AI component, but not every hiring manager knows exactly what they want. The reader should expect
to be asked about their AI experience, and should have their own questions ready about how the
team uses AI and what its plans are.

The book's stance is the one to coach toward: you cannot ignore AI, so stay informed, test the
tools yourself, stay curious, and have an opinion. Treat AI like an intern: it can do some tasks,
but it needs guidance and review.

## What this skill produces

**An inventory of real experience.** Before drafting anything, the skill asks what the reader has
actually done: which tools, on which tasks, what worked, what failed, how they reviewed the output,
and any limits their workplace set. If their experience is thin, it says so and suggests one small,
real project to try before the interview, rather than padding.

**Practice answers** to the questions they are likely to hear:

- How do you use AI in your work today?
- Tell me about a time AI got something wrong. How did you catch it?
- How do you decide what to hand to AI and what to do yourself?
- How do you review AI-generated code?
- What do you think AI will change about this role?

Each answer is short, specific, and built from the inventory, with one concrete example and one
honest limitation. An answer with no limitation sounds like hype.

**Questions to ask the team:**

- How is the team using AI today, and on which parts of the work?
- What tools are approved, and what is off limits for security or legal reasons?
- How do you review AI-generated work before it ships?
- What are your plans for AI over the next year?
- How has it changed what you expect from a senior engineer here?

## Prompt the reader can run directly

> I am preparing for senior software engineer interviews. Here is how I actually use AI at work:
> [TOOLS, TASKS, WHAT WORKED, WHAT FAILED, HOW I REVIEW ITS OUTPUT, ANY LIMITS AT MY COMPANY].
>
> Draft short, honest practice answers to likely interview questions about my AI experience, each
> with one specific example from what I told you and one honest limitation. Do not invent
> experience I did not describe; if something is thin, tell me what small project would give me a
> real example. Then give me five questions I can ask the team about how they use AI and what they
> plan to do with it.

## What to tell the reader

- **Rewrite every answer in your own words.** An answer that sounds like AI wrote it is the worst
  possible answer to this question.
- **Never claim experience you do not have.** A follow-up question will find it.
- **Have an opinion.** "It is an intern that needs review" is more convincing than enthusiasm or
  dismissal.
- **Don't share confidential details** about your current employer's tools or code when giving
  examples.
- **Listen to their answers.** How a team talks about AI tells you a lot about how it reviews work.

## What this skill does not do

It does not run a mock interview or assess the reader's skills. It turns real experience into clear
answers and gives the reader good questions to ask back.
